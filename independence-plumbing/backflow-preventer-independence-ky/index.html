    Frequently Asked Questions About Backflow Preventer in Independence

    Annual residential backflow testing typically costs $75 to $150 per assembly. Repairs with a rebuild kit usually run $150 to $450. New residential installation ranges from $300 to $1,200, and commercial RPZ assemblies often cost $1,500 to $5,000 installed.

    Backflow preventers stop contaminated water from flowing backward into the public supply during pressure drops. Irrigation, fire lines and commercial equipment are frequent sources of contamination. Water providers require them to protect public health.

    Signs of a failing backflow preventer include discolored water, unusual tastes or odors in your water, water leaking from the relief valve, or a sudden drop in water pressure. If you notice any of these issues, it is important to have your device inspected promptly.

    BFK Plumbing services all major types of backflow preventers, including Reduced Pressure Zone Assemblies, Double Check Valve Assemblies, Pressure Vacuum Breakers, and Atmospheric Vacuum Breakers. We work with popular brands like Watts, Wilkins, Febco, and Conbraco.

    Constant dripping from the relief valve, low water pressure and visible cracks are typical signs. Discolored water or a failed test notice also indicate a problem. Prompt repair keeps you compliant.

    The lifespan of a backflow preventer typically ranges from 10 to 20 years, depending on the type, manufacturer, water quality, and maintenance. Regular testing and timely repairs can extend its life. However, certain components like rubber seals may need replacement sooner due to wear and tear.

    Most municipalities, including Independence, require backflow preventers to be tested annually. This regular testing makes sure the device is working correctly and continues to protect your potable water supply from contamination, as required by local water authorities.

    Backflow prevention is essential because of changing water pressures, main breaks and fire flow that can drop pressure suddenly, and the potential for cross-connections from irrigation systems or industrial processes. These factors increase the risk of contaminated water entering the public supply, making a working backflow preventer essential.
